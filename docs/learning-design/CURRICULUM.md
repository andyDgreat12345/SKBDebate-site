# First course: Speak clearly, listen fairly

Original SKB curriculum proposal, English, beginners across formats. Awaiting founder and independent educator review. Assumed secondary-school age; validate before pilot. No prior debate terminology required.

## Outcomes and progression

At the end, a learner should deliver a 90-second contribution with a clear purpose and supported point, paraphrase a different view, answer one relevant question, and revise one part with a stated reason. This is an introductory performance goal, not competition readiness or certification.

Four weeks; two studios per week. Each studio takes an estimated 12–20 minutes; workshops take 45 minutes. Reading speed, accommodations, and repeated attempts change the time. Learners may explore freely; recommendations depend on demonstrated work, not time watched.

| Studio | Observable objective | Motion used to explain | Student performance | Common difficulty and response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Give your listener a destination | State a purpose and one understandable main point for a named audience | A wandering speech becomes a short route with an explicit destination | Give a 30-second explanation of a familiar activity to a newcomer; listener states the main point | Topic mistaken for point → compare a topic label with a complete message |
| 2. Make the reason audible | Connect a relevant reason to a claim in ordinary language | Statement cards separate, an incomplete connection appears, then the student supplies it | Make a 45-second proposal with a reason and connection | Restating a claim as its own reason → contrasting examples and one targeted hint |
| 3. Use support honestly | Distinguish illustration from evidence and avoid overstating a source | A source card retains its scope while an exaggerated claim grows beyond it | Explain what a short reviewed source supports, what it does not, and name it aloud | Anecdote treated as prevalence → request a more limited claim; no invented statistics |
| 4. Show that you heard | Paraphrase another speaker's position without adding claims | Two speech summaries align; added or missing ideas remain visible | Listen to 30 seconds, give a 20-second paraphrase, then ask for confirmation | Response replaces paraphrase → try a second listening turn before objecting |
| 5. Disagree at the right point | Identify one relevant objection while preserving the other's actual claim | Highlight the contested reason or connection; show the rest unchanged | Give a 45-second response containing fair restatement, objection, and why it matters | Opposite opinion without engagement → point to the exact statement being challenged |
| 6. Ask a question that helps | Ask one answerable clarification and use the answer | A question tree opens only after the response; a leading assumption is exposed | Two-turn dialogue: ask, listen, then accurately explain what the answer establishes | Rapid-fire interrogation → practice one question and a full listening turn |
| 7. Explain what matters | Make a value criterion explicit and acknowledge a trade-off | Two options viewed through different criteria; no fake numerical precision | Compare proposals using a stated criterion and acknowledge a reasonable competing value | Personal preference presented as universal fact → explain who is affected and why the criterion matters |
| 8. Speak, respond, revise | Integrate structure, support, listening, and revision | Speech outline and response path; highlight an actual revision | 90-second speech, 30-second question, 30-second answer, revised closing | Trying to fix everything → prioritize one reasoning and one communication change |

Delivery is practiced from studio 1 onward: audible pacing, signposts, pauses, and audience understanding. Feedback does not reward a particular accent, forced eye contact, or a single bodily style. Text-only learners can practice reasoning and structure; the app must not infer delivery competence from typed work.

Dependencies: 1 → 2 → 3; 1 → 4; 2 + 4 → 5; 4 → 6; 2 + 3 → 7; all core practice → 8. A learner who struggles with listening gets studio 4 practice rather than more argument terminology.

## A studio from the learner's perspective

1. **A reason to care:** one concrete communication problem, the goal, estimated time, and an accessible alternative.
2. **An attempt:** a short task with no model answer visible. Explain that this is for comparison, not a score.
3. **A modeled example:** 45–90 seconds of authored explanation. Narration and motion point at the same idea.
4. **A meaningful decision:** identify a main point, connect a reason, or paraphrase. Do not require trivia about the video.
5. **Responsive support:** an authored hint or a bounded AI explanation based on the student's actual response.
6. **Independent performance:** a fresh but comparable task; hints are available, and their use is recorded.
7. **Revision:** student edits or rerecords one part and explains the choice.
8. **Return later:** a short reminder exercise several days later. Timing is a pilot scheduling choice, not an asserted optimum.

A correct recognition answer never certifies an open-ended skill. A reasonable alternative interpretation can be accepted and discussed. Learners can always see why feedback was given and report a mistake.

## Interaction that changes learning

| Student behavior | Teacher response | What changes on screen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gives the topic but no message | Ask what the listener should understand by the end | Topic card remains; main-point slot receives focus |
| Repeats the claim as support | Explain the repetition, then request a separate reason | Repeated phrases align to show the circularity |
| Offers a relevant reason but omits the connection | Ask how that reason supports the particular proposal | One connection is highlighted; no unrelated map rewrite |
| Gives a defensible alternative | Acknowledge it and ask for its justification | A second interpretation is added with a label |
| Requests evidence the course does not contain | Say the source is unavailable and suggest what to look for | Source-needed marker; no fabricated citation |
| Wants an easier explanation | Use a shorter original example, then ask the learner to explain it back | Fewer objects; vocabulary help |
| Asks for the whole answer | Offer a scaffold; learner can explicitly choose to view a model afterward | Mark the attempt as assisted so it is not used as independent evidence |
| Challenges the teacher | Recheck against approved context; admit ambiguity or route to review | Visible correction/report option |

The teacher should not invent psychological diagnoses, personality judgments, or confidence scores from voice. A paused interaction resumes only after the learner chooses to continue.

## Website information architecture

### Public pages

- **Home:** who this is for, one playable sample, course outcomes, founder role, honest development status.
- **Course:** eight studios, prerequisites and estimated practice, availability, sources and reviewers. Do not show unfinished studios as playable.
- **Resources:** original or permitted handouts; external links labeled as external; filters by skill and experience.
- **About and teaching approach:** pedagogical decisions, AI use, content contributors, corrections process, and contact route.

### Learner space

- **Learn:** the next recommended practice, not an overwhelming catalogue. Guest sample first; account needed for durable saving.
- **Studio player:** central visual, captions/transcript, pause/replay, ask/explain controls, exercise panel. On small screens, switch panels without losing position.
- **Practice:** drill selector tied to the current skill; spoken practice can initially happen live with the facilitator.
- **Portfolio:** original response, feedback provenance, revision, assisted/independent status, and reflection. Private by default.
- **Workshops:** cohort schedule with timezone, preparation task, actual facilitator, and access instructions. Only advertise sessions that someone will run.

### Educator space

Assigned learner work, reviewer queue, lesson version history, unresolved feedback reports, source permissions, and cohort attendance. A role is not granted simply by making an account. Start with a minimal private interface and repository-managed content.

## Teacher and learner roles

The founder owns the learning goals and live sessions. An experienced reviewer checks scripts and sample feedback. Peer mentors use a defined review guide and see only work shared with their cohort; learners do not get unsolicited public critique. The AI gives provisional formative support and identifies its basis.

A future community can allow resource submissions with rights declarations, review before publishing, author credit, and reporting. Begin there rather than open direct messaging. Build event-specific pathways after the foundation has been tested with students from more than one speech/debate interest.

## Human workshop run sheet (45 minutes)

- 0–5: welcome, low-pressure warm-up, and reminder of respectful feedback.
- 5–10: revisit a skill without replaying the answer immediately.
- 10–18: founder models a task and thinks aloud about a revision.
- 18–30: pairs rotate speaker/listener; listener paraphrases before giving feedback.
- 30–40: retry with one chosen improvement; facilitator coaches selected difficulties.
- 40–45: independent exit task and next practice instruction.

Offer a nonpublic participation option. Use a supervised partner setting; recording is not necessary for every session. If no workshop facilitator is available, publish the resource as self-study with its actual support level clearly stated.
