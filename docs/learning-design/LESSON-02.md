# Worked lesson 02: Make the reason audible

Original draft for educator review, not a finished video or an adapted third-party lesson. General argument concepts are informed by the source research; this script, fictional examples, and tasks are newly written. Later adaptation must name any source expression actually used.

## Instructional specification

Audience: beginner who can state a main point, after studio 1. Goal: deliver a proposal containing a relevant reason and explain the connection in plain language. No causal-inference theory or fallacy vocabulary required.

Evidence of success: a listener can accurately identify the proposal and explain why the stated reason supports it. Saying “because” alone is not success.

Materials: one illustrative animation with a text equivalent; one model speech; two matched practice prompts; optional audio capture; three feedback branches; a rubric. Fictional premises are labeled as such. They are practice conditions, not real-world evidence.

## Before teaching (about 2 minutes)

Prompt A: “In this fictional school, students waiting for an after-school bus have nowhere to sit. Propose one small change. Explain one reason for it and how that reason supports your change.”

Learner speaks for 30–45 seconds to a partner or writes a response. The partner records the proposal and reason they understood. Preserve the response before any hints. Do not require one predetermined proposal: seating, an accessible waiting room, or a different arrangement could all be defensible.

## Storyboard: about 3 minutes, excluding learner pauses

Exact duration follows recorded narration, not a forced timer. Below is an editorial sequence to record and refine; do not stretch a short sentence to fill a prescribed time slot.

| Scene | Original narration draft | Motion and interaction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. A proposal needs a reason | “Imagine someone says, ‘Our school should open a quiet room during lunch.’ You understand the proposal. But you may still ask why the school should do it. A clear speaker helps the listener follow that next step.” | Show a school outline, then a single proposal card. A listener's “Why?” appears. No decorative stock footage. |
| 2. Repetition does not add support | “Now they add, ‘because having a quiet room is a good idea.’ That repeats their approval. It does not yet tell us what need the room would meet. We need a reason that adds something the listener can examine.” | Put the claim and repeated approval side by side; align the duplicated meaning. Pause: choose whether this adds a separate reason, with an explanation field. |
| 3. Introduce a relevant reason | “Here is an invented detail for our example: students who need a quiet place cannot use the classrooms during lunch. That gives us a possible reason. But the listener still needs to understand how opening this particular room would help.” | Add a clearly marked fictional-scenario card. Reveal a gap between the need and the proposal. |
| 4. Explain the connection | “We could say, ‘Opening the room would give those students a place to work during the time when their classrooms are unavailable.’ Now the connection is easier to follow. That does not settle every question. The school would still need to consider staffing, access, and how the space is used.” | Learner chooses or writes a connecting sentence. Reveal the bridge only after an attempt. Add a small “questions still open” card rather than a victory badge. |
| 5. Hear the complete version | “Listen to the three parts together: ‘Our school should open a quiet room during lunch. Some students need somewhere quiet, but classrooms are unavailable then. Opening this room would give them access during that gap.’ The proposal says what to do. The reason identifies a need. The connection explains why the proposal could address it.” | Highlight each part as spoken. Learner can replay one sentence. Labels identify roles without requiring memorized terminology. |
| 6. Your turn | “Choose a proposal of your own. Give your listener one reason, then explain how it supports the proposal. Ask the listener to tell you what they understood. If their explanation differs from yours, revise the part that was unclear.” | Return to the learner's work. Offer one hint at a time. A visible sources/fiction label stays available. |

On pause, retain the scene and stop narration. On an open question, offer a small explanation in the same diagram; avoid rebuilding the whole lesson. On return, the learner chooses to replay the relevant sentence or continue. Captions are available throughout; reduced-motion mode reveals the same sequence without moving objects.

## Checkpoint and feedback specification

Question: “Which sentence best adds a connection between unavailable classrooms and opening a quiet room?”

- “Quiet rooms are quiet.” → Repeats a property; ask which unmet need the proposal addresses.
- “The room would provide a place to work during the time classrooms are closed.” → Relevant connection; ask what practical question remains.
- “Everyone who uses it will earn higher grades.” → Introduces an unsupported outcome; ask for a narrower statement using only the scenario.
- “Another answer” → Accept free text. Consider alternatives such as access for a specific group; do not reject because wording differs.

These authored paths give predictable support. The live tutor may rephrase, ask a clarifying question, or point at a relevant card, but cannot invent a study or label the policy proven.

Example tutor exchange:

Student: “So it is definitely the best option?”

Teacher: “We have shown one reason it might help. We have not compared it with other options or checked the costs. What alternative would you want to compare?”

Student: “Could students just use the library?”

Teacher: “Yes, that is a reasonable alternative to investigate. The scenario does not say whether the library is available. How would you ask about that without assuming the answer?”

## Independent task and revision

Prompt B: “In this fictional school, students finishing physical education have a long walk to the nearest drinking-water refill point. Propose one small change. Explain one reason for it and how that reason supports your change.”

Use the same instructions and preparation time as Prompt A. The learner gives 30–45 seconds. No model answer or AI help appears until they submit, unless they choose assistance; assisted attempts are labeled accordingly.

Then the listener paraphrases. The student selects one unclear connection and revises it. Ask: “What did you change so that your listener could follow your reasoning?”

Prompts A and B are proposed parallel tasks, not validated equivalent assessments. Counterbalance their order in a small pilot where practical and check topic familiarity. Neither asks the learner to diagnose selection bias, unlike the previous cycling example.

## Review rubric (human-calibrated, not an automatic grade)

| Dimension | 0: not yet shown | 1: partly shown | 2: clearly shown |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Main point | No identifiable proposal | Proposal discernible but vague | Listener can state the proposal accurately |
| Support | Repetition or unrelated statement | Relevant reason with missing detail | Relevant reason grounded in the stated scenario |
| Connection | No explanation of relevance | Connection implied or ambiguous | Explains how the reason supports the proposal |
| Scope | Invents evidence or unsupported certainty | Meaning or extent needs clarification | Stays within the scenario without unsupported claims |

A beginner does not have to list every limitation to meet the scope criterion. Clear reasoning in nonstandard English can satisfy the same criterion as polished English.

Keep delivery observations separate: can the listener follow the structure; where would a pause or signpost help? Do not award a delivery score from text or use accent as a proxy for clarity. The rubric describes this task; it is not a validated measure of general critical thinking.

For facilitator calibration, compare: “Do it because it is good” (repetition); “Do it because rooms are closed” (relevant but incomplete connection); “Make the room available at lunch so students have a quiet place while classrooms are closed” (clear link, practical details still open). Review justified alternative proposals on their reasoning, not agreement with the policy.

## Production and pilot gates

Before animation: founder checks natural phrasing; another educator checks alternative answers and scope; three beginners try the paper/spoken activity. Record where instructions fail.

Before release: every checkpoint works with text only; labels remain readable on iPad; narration can stop; learner work survives approved navigation/save behavior; wrong answers lead to explanation rather than lockout; transcript and audio match; no third-party voice or media rights are assumed.
